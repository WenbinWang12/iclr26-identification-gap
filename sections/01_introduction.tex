\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A model that learns fifteen tasks in sequence under one fixed LoRA adapter forgets.
The standard reading is that capacity ran out: the adapter's rank cannot hold
fifteen tasks' worth of representation change, so methods allocate rank more
cleverly, penalise interference between task subspaces, or compress and retrieve old
subspaces. All of this treats forgetting as a property of the parameter update.

Part of it is not. When a prompted model is scored by restricted argmax over a
verbalizer set (\texttt{False}/\texttt{True}, \texttt{Bad}/\texttt{Good}), the
decision depends on the \emph{difference} between the label logits, and that
difference can be corrected by a per-label additive offset. An offset has zero rank.
It costs a handful of floats. If a task's accuracy can be restored by one, then
whatever was lost was never a capacity problem, and rank spent on it is wasted.

This says the fixed budget faces \emph{two} capacity constraints, in two different
places: an output-layer one that an offset can fix without spending rank, and a
representation-layer one that it cannot. This paper measures how much of the
forgetting on a standard $15$-task stream is of each kind, and proposes a method that
relieves both at a fixed parameter budget: reallocating the rank across task
families for the representation, and adding a per-scope offset for the output. At a
bit-identical budget the two moves compose, beating the standard single-adapter
baseline end-to-end by $+11.76$~pp across three seeds (Section~\ref{sec:combined}).

\paragraph{The term, and a boundary condition that makes it falsifiable.}
Write $R_g^{\mathrm{orc}}$ for task $g$'s accuracy under the best offset chosen
\emph{knowing $g$}, and $R_g^{\mathrm{shr}}$ for the best offset shared across all
tasks. The gap $\Delta^{\mathrm{id}}_g = R_g^{\mathrm{orc}} - R_g^{\mathrm{shr}}$ is
what task identity would buy at the output layer, and it is separate from
representation drift by construction. It also has a sharp boundary: if tasks'
verbalizer sets are pairwise disjoint, a single offset can serve every task at once
and $\Delta^{\mathrm{id}} \equiv 0$ exactly (Proposition~\ref{prop:disjoint}). The
term exists only where output tokens are \emph{shared}, and on Order-4 ten of
fifteen tasks share theirs. We check the zero prediction in every run: $0$
violations, worst deviation $0.0$.

\paragraph{What we measure.}
Under one shared offset the gap is $3.13$~pp. Under an offset shared only within a
\emph{scope} (the set of tasks with the same verbalizer, readable from the prompt
without knowing which task is being asked) it is $0.00$~pp, CI $[-1.71,+1.71]$.
The scope index recovers $+1.51$~pp, CI $[+0.43,+2.74]$; adding true task identity
on top buys a further $+0.02$~pp pooled, but that pooled figure averages over
singleton scopes where our implementation makes the two rungs identical by
construction, and on the multi-task scopes where the comparison is live task
identity buys $+1.10$~pp, CI $[+0.40,+1.83]$
(Section~\ref{sec:gap-real}, disclosure~(iii)). So most of what the output layer is
missing is the scope, which is free at inference time; the residual attributable to
the task label is small but not zero, and we do not claim it vanishes.

\paragraph{The output layer is only half of it.}
Fixing the output layer does not stop the representation from forgetting. We froze
three parameter-space protocols that target the representation --- an orthogonality
penalty of O-LoRA's form, anchoring the verbalizer logits to their post-training
values, and anchoring them in the gauge-fixed coordinates the theory identifies ---
and none improved task-agnostic accuracy; at the strengths their own gates selected,
two reduced it. The per-scope offset kept recovering the same $+1.51$~pp on top of
each, confirming the two layers are separate, but none of these \emph{objective}
changes bought representation-layer accuracy. So one shared adapter faces two
distinct capacity constraints, and penalising interference after the fact did not
relieve the second one.

\paragraph{The method: relieve both constraints at a fixed budget.}
We stop changing the objective and change the \emph{allocation}. Split the tasks
into $K{=}4$ semantic families and give each a rank-$2$ adapter; the four hold
exactly the parameters of one shared rank-$8$ adapter, so this is a reallocation and
not extra capacity. Each family adapter is perturbed only by its own family, which
contains the cross-family overwriting that a shared adapter suffers. On top of that
we apply the per-scope output offset. At a bit-identical budget, on three seeds, the
grouped allocation beats the shared adapter by $+8.00$~pp, the offset adds on top,
and the combined method beats the standard shared/no-offset baseline
\emph{end-to-end by $+11.76$~pp}, positive on every seed (Section~\ref{sec:combined}).
The two moves are independent: grouping still adds $+5.18$~pp after the output layer
is corrected, so neither subsumes the other. This is the paper's central positive
result: two capacity constraints, two matched-budget moves, and the relief adds.

\paragraph{What we report as an upper bound, and why.}
Both moves are scored at their upper bound: the offset is fit against the current
model, and family routing uses the scope at evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:enumeration}
enumerates the deployable sources for the offset (there are exactly two) and
Sections~\ref{sec:sso} and~\ref{sec:bpo} show each has a cost on this stream (a
stored offset goes stale, $5.08$~pp below floor; a query-batch offset gains
$+4.40$~pp then inverts to $-0.67$~pp under a class-mix control). We report those
honestly and note that grouping is positioned to soften the staleness cost, because
a per-family adapter drifts less than a monolithic one, a mechanism we motivate
and mark as measured only at the upper bound (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The
allocation claim itself (two matched-budget moves that compose) does not depend
on how tightly the deployable offset tracks the upper bound.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item A decomposition separating the identification gap (output layer) from
  representation drift, with an exactly-zero boundary condition
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:disjoint}) that makes the output term falsifiable, and a
  minimax characterisation of the best shared offset as a Chebyshev radius
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:cheb}). The budgeted extension is proved for binary shared
  verbalizers and \textbf{open} beyond them; we state which numbers rest on which
  (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
\item A fixed-budget two-constraint method: task-family grouping adapters
  (representation) plus a per-scope offset (output), each at a bit-identical
  parameter count to a shared adapter (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item The measurement that makes it a method and not a definition. On Order-4, at a
  matched budget and across three seeds: grouping alone $+8.00$~pp, offset alone
  $+6.58$~pp, grouping-on-top-of-offset $+5.18$~pp, and end-to-end $+11.76$~pp over
  the standard baseline, all positive on every seed (Section~\ref{sec:combined}).
  The two moves are independent and additive, and the one task that loses under
  grouping (the $14$-way DBpedia, rank $2$ too small) is reported, not averaged
  away.
\item Evidence that the objective is the wrong lever for the representation
  constraint: three frozen parameter-space interventions fail to buy
  task-agnostic accuracy, motivating the allocation move
  (Section~\ref{sec:three-anchors}).
\item An enumeration of the deployable sources for the output offset --- exactly two
  non-vacuous rules (Section~\ref{sec:enumeration}) --- with each tested and its cost
  on this stream measured: a stored offset goes stale ($5.08$~pp below floor, with
  singleton scopes locating the cause, Section~\ref{sec:sso}), and a query-batch
  offset inverts under a pre-registered class-mix control (Section~\ref{sec:bpo}).
  This is why the method reports the offset at its upper bound and states the
  deployment gap plainly.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{How to read the evidence.}
Every phase was frozen with its criteria, thresholds, and predeclared outcomes
before it was implemented, each protocol SHA-pinned; we report the criteria that
failed alongside those that passed, including one we had predicted would fail. All
comparisons are internal (arms differing in one component at an identical
budget, identical splits, identical seeds) and the official test files are never
opened, so our absolute accuracies are not comparable to published leaderboard
numbers and we do not place them in such tables. We run no published continual-PEFT
baseline, and make no claim against one.
